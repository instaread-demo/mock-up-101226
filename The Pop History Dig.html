“Blast Kills Campers”
El Paso Pipeline: Aug 2000

August 31, 2026 J.D.

On Friday evening, August 18th, 2000, three families from Carlsbad, New Mexico headed out for some weekend camping and fishing to an area not far away along the Pecos River in southeastern New Mexico. They traveled in three separate vehicles. There were twelve people altogether – the Heady, Smith and Sumler families; seven adults, three children, and two infants. The families were related by marriage and shared grandchildren. Later, as the families began their camping along the Pecos River – and unbeknownst to them – they had chosen a spot near to where a buried natural gas pipeline lay. In fact, they had driven their pick-up trucks and other vehicles unwittingly across the buried pipeline to reach their campsite — an unmarked site, popular with fishermen, according to some local police.


Map shows location and route of the interstate El Paso Natural Gas Line 1103 and location of the Pecos River Station site where the visiting families from Carlsbad, NM had set up their campsite and where the August 2000 pipeline explosion occurred.

The campsite was adjacent to a steel and concrete service bridge that crossed the Pecos River. The bridge also carried two natural gas pipelines across the river. Nearby were two additional steel suspension bridges also carrying pipelines that crossed the river. The main service bridge was roped off at both ends and a “Private Right of Way No Trespassing” sign was posted at each entrance, indicating restricted access. Two “Caution High Pressure Gas Line” signs were posted near the east entrance to the service bridge. Also, another “Warning-No Trespassing” sign was posted alongside the right-of-way road (near the intersection with the county road) on the east side of the river. This sign read as follows: “This road and right of way is private property and is not for public use. This pipeline carries natural gas under high pressure and is dangerous. All persons are warned of the danger to person and property. KEEP OFF.”


Overview of blast area: the families camping had located between the rupture site and the Pecos River. The two bridge towers at left, each 85 feet tall, also carried pipelines. The effects of the explosion would spread over the entire site.

It is not clear if any in the camping party had read the warning signs, ignored them, or thought they applied only to the bridge and service road, and not the adjacent areas. At the time of the accident, a private landowner owned the accident site. After the accident, El Paso purchased this property and installed fencing to restrict access. The general area at the time was open rangeland, with the nearest homes 10 to 20 miles away.

In any case, that Friday evening, the families settled into their campsite. Then at about 5:30 a.m. the next morning, still in darkness as the families slept, the buried 30-inch diameter El Paso Natural Gas pipeline ruptured and exploded. An enormous fireball covered the entire area, shooting flames later estimated to have reached nearly 500 feet and visible for miles.


August 2000 explosion & fire of El Paso Natural Gas pipeline in New Mexico that killed 12 people. At lower left of towering fireball flame, the feint outlines of the two 85-foot-tall suspension bridge structures can be seen.

There were three explosions at the site: the first tearing open the pipeline, the second igniting the escaping gas, and a third smaller explosion. A National Transportation Safety Board official at the site a few days later described the ruptured pipeline as “a massive flame-thrower that showered burning fuel on the victims.” The explosion generated heat so intense, he explained, “that sand melted into glass and concrete turned to powder…” Seismic monitoring instruments some distance away registered the explosions. The fire burned for almost an hour before firefighters brought it under control. The firefighters who had reached the campsite about an hour after the explosions found evidence of campfires and gasoline lanterns near the campers’ trucks that possibly could have been ignition sources, but no one knows for sure.

Firefighters came from Carlsbad and the towns of Otis, Joel, and Loving to fight the blaze. A few moved to within about a half-mile of the fire but retreated because of the heat, even at that distance. “We saw it was going to melt the paint off our trucks,” said one firefighter. The firefighters, however, had no idea that campers were at the site. After the gas was turned off and the flames died down,“We saw it was going to melt the paint off our trucks,” said one retreating firefighter of the heat coming from the blaze, a half-mile away. the firefighters thought they would just have to put out a few scattered grass fires and go home. But that’s when they heard the screams from the direction of the river.

The explosion and fireball would eventually take the lives of all 12 of the campers. However, six of the party — three women, two men, and a little girl — had made it to the river and tried to swim away from the intense heat. They made it about 300 yards downstream.

Firefighters and emergency crews hearing the screams ran across several hundred yards of rocky desert carrying their medical gear, and descended into a dry arroyo with steep, 10-foot banks that ran beside the river. On the river bed they found the six survivors who were “hideously burned” and “looked like mummies.” On the river bed they found six survivors who were “hideously burned” and “looked like mummies.” They were still alive. They were still alive. One of the party, a young mother, Amy Heady, was lying next to her husband, Royle Dale Heady, 20. Amy said she wanted to see her babies. Her husband raised his head and looked over at her and said they were all dead.

Back at the campsite, firefighters did find the bodies of three young children with the residue of a playpen melted around them. On the river bank, Royle and Amy Heady were dying. The emergency responders did what they could. Three others in that group died later that day, and one young girl – who emergency workers managed to get to an ambulance, later receiving a series of hospital burn treatments – lived another 10 days before succumbing.


Burnt out vehicles of the campers near their campsite.

About a hundred yards away from where the campers had settled, a huge crater formed where the pipe had blown out. A 50 foot section of the pipeline was ejected in three pieces. Two of the pieces were thrown more that 225 feet, toward the river. One hit the cables that supported the pipeline suspension bridges. These bridges were also partly burned as were the pipelines they carried – one active line and one out-of-service line. Both of the pipelines fell from the structures to the riverbanks but did not leak. The campers’ three vehicles and camping equipment were burnt out and destroyed, and vegetation along both riverbanks was burned.


Enormous crater in the earth where the El Paso pipeline exploded, August 2000, missing the 50-foot section of pipe blown out in multiple pieces during explosion.

In May 2000 – before the Carlsbad explosion – El Paso Energy Pipeline Group President John W. Somerhalder II, had testified before the U.S. Senate Energy and Transportation Committee, then urging the committee not to approve legislation that proposed to increase safety fines. He argued against a heavy reliance on increased safety fines, stating that higher financial penalties did not inherently improve public safety or prevent operational failures. “El Paso Energy complies with the pipeline safety regulations, because they are the law and because we believe that in most cases they increase pipeline safety” Somerhalder explained in his remarks. “ El Paso Energy’s goal is to increase pipeline safety, not avoid penalties.” Somerhalder also told the committee, “We are continuously working to improve our record of safety, for, like you, we believe one fatality is one too many.”


NTSB, Pipeline Accident Report, “Natural Gas Pipeline Rupture and Fire Near Carlsbad, New Mexico, August 19, 2000,” adopted, February 11, 2003. Click for Amazon.
But less than three months later, in the aftermath of the Carlsbad explosion, El Paso’s shortcomings had become clear, as the Federal agencies reviewing that incident would find. In fact, within days of the incident, on August 23, 2000, the U. S. Office of Pipeline Safety (OPS) issued a corrective action order to El Paso requiring actions in 25 specific areas to protect the public and environment from potential hazards. A year later, in August 2001, OPS levied a $2.52 million civil penalty on El Paso for its violations of pipeline safety law.

The NTSB, meanwhile, would determine in its final report that the probable cause of the pipeline explosion and fire was “a significant reduction in pipe wall thickness due to severe internal corrosion.” This severe corrosion occurred, according to the NTSB, because El Paso’s corrosion control program “failed to prevent, detect, or control” internal corrosion within the pipeline. Contributing to the accident, said the NTSB, were “ineffective Federal pre-accident inspections…that did not identify deficiencies in the company’s internal corrosion control program.”

In July 2001, Austin American -Statesman newspaper reporters Jeff Nesmith and Ralph Haurwitz reporting on the Carlsbad explosion and pipeline safety generally, noted: “Federal pipeline safety records contain scores of cases in which local gas companies and interstate transporters such as El Paso Natural Gas have reported temporarily shutting down pipelines because of corrosion.” Pipeline corrosion, in fact, remains a major concern throughout the North American oil and gas pipeline system today. Nesmith and Haurwitz had also found that El Paso’s Pecos River pipeline had been given a clean bill of health in an inspection less than a month before the explosion, but had not had more thorough hydrostatic testing in more than 50 years.

In July 2007, El Paso agreed to pay a $15.5 million fine as part of a settlement with the U. S. Department of Justice and pipeline safety regulators related to the Carlsbad explosion. The Department had declined to bring a criminal action. Wrongful death actions were also brought against El Paso by families related to the victims in 2000 and 2002, and settlements were reached in all cases.


Map used by the Austin American Statesman newspaper showing fatalities by state from pipeline incidents, 1984-2000.

There are 2.6 million miles of natural gas pipelines in the U.S. This includes 2.3 million miles of gas distribution and over 300,000 miles of gas transmission These lines often carry hazardous materials with the potential to cause public injury and environmental damage in rural and urban areas. A U.S. map of deaths by pipeline incidents across the U.S. during the 1984-2000 period (shown above) used by the Austin American-Statesman in its reporting on pipeline safety, showed that fatalities occurred in more than 40 states. El Paso, meanwhile, has had continuing problems with its pipelines through the early 2020s, as shown below.


Leaks, Fires, Explosions

“Other El Paso Incidents”
As Recorded at Wikipedia: 2000-2021

August 28, 2000 – In Denver City, Texas, an El Paso Natural Gas Company 30-inch-diameter (76 cm) pipeline leak was discovered in a farmer’s field after [El Paso] was informed of darkened soil. After the line was excavated, the leak was found at a weld failure where a mechanical clamp repaired an older leak adjacent to the long seam of the pipe, which was manufactured in 1948.[19]

December 4, 2000 – Near Baytown, Texas, an El Paso Field Services 30-inch diameter pipeline ruptured and gas blew into the air. The Beach City fire department reported a plume of natural gas but no fire at the junction of Highway 565 and Needlepoint Road (FM 2354) and partially evacuated the area. The pipe was manufactured and installed in 1955.[19]

August 11, 2001 – At approximately 5:05 a.m. MST, an El Paso Natural Gas 24-inch gas transmission pipeline failed near Williams, Arizona, resulting in the release of natural gas. The natural gas continued to discharge for about an hour before igniting. Stress corrosion cracking was determined to be the cause of the failure.[44][45]

January 6, 2002 – An El Paso Field Services natural gas pipeline was damaged at Schutenburg, Texas, when a log drift created by heavy rains and flooding hit the pipeline, creating longitudinal strain stressing a coupling, which separated. The coupling was located approximately 15′ landward from the riverbank. The pipe depth at the location of the leak was 84 inches.[56]

March 23, 2003 – A 24-inch El Paso Natural Gas pipeline near Eaton, Colorado, exploded. The explosion sent flames 160 meters in the air, forcing evacuations. No one was injured. The heat from the flames melted the siding of two nearby houses and started many smaller grass fires.[83]

November 5, 2009 – Two people were hurt when an El Paso Natural Gas pipeline exploded in the Texas Panhandle near Bushland, Texas. The explosion left a hole about 30 yards by 20 yards and close to 15 feet (4.6 m) deep. The orange inferno rose about 700 feet in the air; the blast incinerated the home of the Jose Torres family, injuring his wife Agnieszka and daughter Franczeska. About 200 residents in the area were evacuated. Bushland is in Potter County, about 15 miles (24 km) west of Amarillo. The failure was in an abandoned tap, but the exact failure reason remains unknown. The explosion cause $436,136 in property damage.[266][267][268][269]

December 1, 2009 – “El Paso Corp. Fined $2.3 million For Wyoming Pipeline Explosion”. Huffington Post. December 1, 2009.

June 10, 2015 – Kinder Morgan’s El Paso Natural Gas control/relief equipment failed and leaked in Gray County, Texas. Technicians believed debris or trash may have caused the valve to malfunction.[303]

August 18. 2020 – An El Paso Natural Gas transmission pipeline exploded and burned near Midland, Texas. There were no injuries

August 15. 2021 – An El Paso Natural Gas gas transmission pipeline exploded and burned in Coolidge, Arizona. 2 people were killed and another was seriously burned.[24]
