Landlords will face fines for breaching confidentiality

Change aims to protect tenants who break leases because of domestic violence

By: Carol Sanders Posted: 5:04 PM CDT Friday, Oct. 9, 2026

The province is amending legislation to allow landlords to be penalized if they breach the confidentiality of tenants who break their lease by obtaining a domestic violence certificate.

“Hopefully, now there’s going to be measures in place so that if this happens again, and God forbid it happens again to anyone else, that there would be penalties imposed on landlords who do that,” said one victim whose concerns were published on the front page of the Free Press last month.

“Hopefully, now there’s going to be measures in place so that if this happens again… that there would be penalties imposed on landlords who do that.”

Under current legislation, landlords must ensure that any information received from those tenants regarding the termination of the tenancy is kept confidential, but there are no penalties for landlords who violate that section of the act. After hearing from victims put at risk when landlords broke the law by violating their confidentiality, the government announced Monday that it is amending the Residential Tenancies Act to allow for penalties.

The young woman who spoke to the Free Press showed that her landlord copied her alleged abuser in email correspondence that included her personal email address and the domestic violence certificate she obtained in 2025 to end a tenancy agreement early and leave an abusive relationship.

In 2019, the Residential Tenancies Act was amended to allow someone experiencing domestic violence or stalking to end a lease early by obtaining a domestic violence certificate.

Rather than requiring the tenant to go to court to obtain an order, the legislation allows a physician, psychologist, social worker or shelter employee to provide confirmation that violence or stalking occurred to Manitoba Justice’s victim services branch, which then issues a certificate that can be used to break a lease.

The section of the act related to domestic violence requires the landlord to ensure that any information received from the tenant regarding the termination of the tenancy is kept confidential.

When the young woman complained about her landlord’s breach to the Residential Tenancies Branch, she learned that there were no penalties for a violation. She contacted her MLA, Cindy Lamoureux (Lib – Tyndall Park), who raised the matter during question period this spring.

At a committee meeting Monday night considering Bill 13, the Residential Tenancies Amendment Act, Public Service Delivery Minister Mintu Sandhu announced an amendment to allow the director of the Residential Tenancies Branch to issue an administrative penalty to a landlord when a breach occurs.

“We don’t want the landlords to provide any personal information to the abusers.”

“We want to make sure we provide protection to the people who have been abused,” Sandhu said at the legislative building. “In the future, we don’t want the landlords to provide any personal information to the abusers.”

The bill, which is on track to become law this session, increases the maximum administrative penalty to $10,000 from $5,000.

Landlords need to know there is a price to pay for violating the confidentiality of a tenant’s domestic violence certificate, the minister responsible for gender equity, Nahanni Fontaine, said Thursday.

“The reality is that people start to respond when there are penalties attached to breaching people’s privacy and putting folks at risk,” Fontaine said in an interview.

“We’ve heard loud and clear in respect of protecting victims of (intimate partner violence), their privacy … and ensuring that they don’t come into contact with their abuser.”

The victim who spoke to the Free Press said she she hopes landlords get the message.

Her MLA said she’s thankful to Sandhu for taking action, and to her constituent for having the courage to raise the issue.

“Because they have spoken up, they have helped shine a light on an important issue that others have experienced,” Lamoureux said Friday.

“Their initiative will help strengthen protections for others going forward.”

Open letter to the premier

An open letter Friday to Premier Wab Kinew and the minister in charge of rent regulations sent by 90 organizations is demanding tighter controls on landlords in Manitoba.

“Tenants and people experiencing housing insecurity are in serious need of greater protections and measures supporting housing affordability,” said the letter to Kinew and Public Service Delivery Minister Mintu Sandhu signed by groups including Acorn Family Place, the Canadian Union of Public Employees, the Right to Housing Coalition and the West Central Women’s Resource Centre.

It asks the NDP government to follow through on changes to rent regulations announced in March and posted on the government’s EngageMB online portal. It invited feedback from Manitobans on increasing the number of rental units subject to rent regulation by increasing the monthly rent exempt threshold to $2,000 from $1,670. It also asked for input on reducing the portions of capital expenditures by 50 per cent that a landlord is entitled to claim as part of an above-guideline rent increase application in a year.

One of Kinew’s 2023 election promises was to “strengthen rent controls with legislation to protect renters from big rate hikes,” the proponents of tougher regulations said in a news release.

The letter-writers say the regulation increasing the number of units covered by control was announced Sept. 3 and takes effect in 2027.

The other major change that would allow for much smaller above-guideline rent increases for capital repairs hasn’t been announced. Its supporters say they’re concerned the NDP government will “cave” to landlord pressure and scrap that change, noting cases where tenants have been hit with above-guideline rent increases of 200 per cent.

“It makes a huge difference for tenants because it will reduce their increases in a significant ways and the landlords will still get all of those costs repaid,” the Right to Housing Coalition’s Yutaka Dirks said Friday.
