Six ways small businesses can improve their AI visibility

For small-business owners, the opportunity is about making sure their business is easy for both people and technology to understand, trust and find.

By SEOOnly October 8, 2026

For a small-business owner, getting found online has traditionally meant worrying about Google.

Is the website ranking? Is the Google Business Profile up to date? Are customers leaving reviews? Does the business appear when someone searches for its services locally?

Those questions still matter.

But there is now another question worth asking: What happens when a potential customer asks an AI assistant about your industry instead?

Consumers can now ask platforms such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini to recommend a service, compare businesses, explain their options or help them decide what to buy.

A homeowner might ask for recommendations for a local builder. A parent might research tutoring services. A business owner could ask for accounting firms suitable for SMEs. Someone considering a cosmetic treatment might ask AI what they should look for in a provider and which businesses offer it nearby.

For small businesses, this creates a new visibility challenge.

You may have a great business, loyal customers and years of experience – but does your digital presence communicate that clearly enough?

This is where AI search optimisation is beginning to matter.

The good news is that improving AI visibility doesn’t necessarily mean starting again. Many of the fundamentals that make a business credible to customers and traditional search engines also help create a clearer digital footprint for emerging search technologies.

Here are six places small-business owners can start.

1. Find out what AI already knows about you

Before changing anything, establish your starting point.

Ask several AI platforms questions a potential customer might realistically ask about your category.

Don’t simply type in the name of your business. That tells you very little.

Instead, try questions such as:

“Can you recommend a [service] in [location]?”

“What are some reputable [type of business] in [location]?”

“Who should I consider for [specific service] near me?”

Look at which competitors appear and how they are described.

Then ask about your own business.

Is the information accurate? Does the platform understand what you actually do? Does it associate you with the right location and services?

This isn’t a scientific ranking test, and AI responses can vary. But it can provide a useful snapshot of how clearly your business exists in the wider digital information environment.

2. Make it incredibly clear what your business does

Small-business websites often assume customers – and technology – understand more than they actually do.

A homepage might say a company provides “solutions for a better tomorrow” without clearly explaining what those solutions are.

That might sound impressive in a brand presentation. It isn’t particularly helpful when something is trying to understand the business.

Your website should clearly answer basic questions:

What do you do? Who do you do it for? Where do you operate? What services do you specialise in? Why should someone trust you?

Each important service should have enough useful information around it to establish what the business actually knows and does.

Clarity isn’t boring.

In search, clarity is an asset.

3. Stop thinking about content as a pile of keywords

Traditional SEO earned itself a reputation for awkward website copy written primarily to squeeze keywords onto a page.

Modern search requires a more useful approach.

Think about the real questions customers ask before they buy.

A mortgage broker hears certain questions repeatedly. So does a dentist, lawyer, builder, accountant or beauty clinic.

Those questions are valuable.

Businesses can build useful content around pricing considerations, common problems, comparisons, processes, misconceptions and the things customers should understand before making a decision.

This creates information that is genuinely useful to potential customers while also providing clearer context about the business’s expertise.

Instead of asking “What keywords should we stuff onto our website?”, ask:

“What does our customer need to know before they choose someone like us?”

That is a much better starting point.

4. Build evidence that your business is credible

Any business can claim to be the best.

Evidence is more persuasive.

Reviews, testimonials, industry credentials, professional memberships, awards, media coverage, detailed team expertise and mentions on credible third-party websites all contribute to the broader picture of a business online.

For local businesses, consistency also matters.

Your business name, location, contact information and service information should not tell conflicting stories across your website, directories, profiles and other online sources.

The objective isn’t to manufacture authority for AI.

It’s to make genuine authority easier to verify.

5. Don’t abandon traditional SEO

One of the biggest mistakes small businesses could make is assuming that AI search means Google SEO no longer matters.

It does.

A technically healthy website, clear site structure, quality content, local search optimisation, authority and good user experience remain important foundations.

The distinction between “traditional SEO” and “AI SEO” may also become less useful over time as search engines introduce more AI features and AI platforms become another part of the customer discovery journey.

For businesses, the sensible strategy is not choosing one over the other.

It’s building a search presence capable of adapting to both.

6. Look at your competitors differently

Most businesses know who their traditional competitors are.

AI creates an interesting new exercise: Find out who technology thinks your competitors are.

Search for your products and services without mentioning your company.

Which businesses keep appearing?

Then investigate why.

Do they have stronger service pages? More useful content? Better reviews? Greater third-party authority? A clearer local presence? More consistent information across the web?

The objective isn’t to copy them.

It’s to identify the signals that may be helping them become more visible.

This is also where AI search becomes commercially interesting for SMEs.

If three competitors are repeatedly appearing when potential customers ask for recommendations and your business isn’t, that visibility gap is worth understanding.

Start with the customer, not the technology

AI search can quickly become a conversation dominated by acronyms, algorithms and technical terminology.

Small-business owners don’t need to become AI experts.

They need to understand how their customers are changing.

SEOOnly, a specialist SEO and AI search visibility company founded by Australian marketing entrepreneur Preeti Kennedy, works with businesses on both traditional search and emerging AI discovery.

Kennedy says the biggest mistake businesses can make is treating AI search as either something to panic about or something they can safely ignore.

“Small businesses don’t need to suddenly optimise for every new piece of technology that appears,” Kennedy said.

“They need strong digital foundations and a very clear answer to a simple question: If someone is looking for exactly what you sell, how easy is it for them – or the technology they’re using – to understand why your business is relevant?”

That distinction is important.

The platforms will continue changing. New AI tools will emerge. Search engines will evolve and consumer habits will shift with them.

Trying to chase every algorithm is unlikely to be a sustainable strategy for a small business.

Building a digital presence that clearly demonstrates what you do, where you do it, what you know and why customers trust you is a much better investment.

Because regardless of whether the next customer finds you through Google, an AI assistant or a platform that hasn’t been invented yet, one principle of marketing isn’t changing: Being a great business only helps if customers can find you.
