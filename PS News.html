Taylor calls on Labor to force ATO to reverse its credit card ban

8 October 2026 | By Chris Johnson
Start the conversation

The Coalition has called on Labor to force the Australian Taxation Office to reverse its decision to refuse credit card payments due to the surcharge ban that has just come into effect.

The ATO isn’t backing down from its decision to stop accepting credit card payments from 1 December.

Instead, it held an emergency meeting on Thursday (7 October) with business groups frustrated by the surcharge ban and what they call the tax office’s hypocrisy in not absorbing the costs.

Opposition Leader Angus Taylor said a meeting wasn’t necessary as the ATO should admit it got it wrong or be forced to back down by the Federal Government.

“They should just reverse the decision; they don’t need a meeting. The government should reverse the decision,” he said.

“The government should instruct the ATO today to reverse that decision. This is a government that says to small businesses, ‘you’ve got to absorb the surcharges’, and then the Australian Tax Office says we’re not going to accept credit card payments because we can’t accept the surcharges.

“I mean, seriously. The hypocrisy of this government knows no bounds. They are out of touch.

“Here we have the Prime Minister over with the Energy Minister at their pre-COP flop in the South Pacific, where it’s so boring the Energy Minister falls asleep at his own conference.

“I mean, seriously. Get back here, sort it out, and reverse it straight away.”

The ATO has said it cannot absorb the costs of credit card charges.

The surcharge ban that came into effect on 1 October covers EFTPOS, Visa and Mastercard and includes credit, debit and prepaid cards.

It was introduced following the RBA’s Review of Merchant Card Payment Costs and Surcharging, which found that removing the surcharges would support a more efficient and competitive payments system.

The ATO responded to the ban by outright refusing to accept credit card payments after 30 November.

It says that as a government agency, it would not be appropriate for the cost of credit card merchant fees to be “transferred to the community”.

“The ATO recognises that some taxpayers currently rely on credit card payments to manage their tax payments and understands this change may require some adjustments,” it said in a statement.

“The ATO is committed to helping taxpayers transition to alternative payment methods and will continue to support those experiencing financial hardship or other circumstances that make it difficult to meet their obligations.”

The Council of Small Business Organisations Australia (COSBOA) was at the snap meeting on Thursday and said the ATO’s approach makes the case that small businesses are being hit with an extra impost because of the surcharge ban.

Small business owners are now expected to absorb card transaction fees but will no longer use credit card payments to the ATO as a cash-flow option.

In a statement, it said the ban “warrants a rethink”, but the bigger issue is the surcharge ban and small businesses’ capacity to absorb those costs.

“The ATO’s own response highlights that concern,” the COSBOA statement said.

Australian Chamber of Commerce and Industry (ACCI) chief executive officer Andrew McKellar was also at the ATO meeting and said there was a “clear double standard” in play.

“Government is telling small business to absorb the hit from the surcharge ban and its cost, while the tax office is saying a different set of rules applies to it,” he said.

“I participated in a meeting earlier today. I mean, it was with ATO officials. The way I’d characterise it is it was an operational meeting.

“They’re not discussing revisiting the decision. They’re really looking at how it is implemented. What are the impacts from a stakeholder point of view, particularly for small businesses? What does it mean, and what do they have to do to comply?

“I think the problem here is, as each day goes by, a little bit more information comes out, there’s a clear double standard. It’s clear that business has been expected to shoulder this, but the tax office isn’t prepared to do so.”

The Australian Retail Council’s CEO Chris Rodwell has added to the calls for the ATO to reverse its decision.

“Removing credit card payments will disrupt how some small businesses manage their cash flow at an already difficult time,” he said.

“Small businesses deserve a fair go. If they are expected to wear these costs, so should the tax office.”

The ATO insists credit card payments make up only 2.3 per cent of payments to the tax office, with more than 60 per cent of credit card payments (by value) made by wealthy privately owned groups and public and multinational businesses.

It says only about 5 per cent of small businesses use credit cards to pay their taxes.
